How the calculator works

How a lever becomes a rank

Every lever runs through the same five steps. Here they are for one real example: raising arts, parks and culture from Jacksonville's 0.079 to the Top 50 typical value of 0.25.

  1. Step 1The value0.079 → 0.25

    Each lever starts from Jacksonville's 2026 value in Oxford's data.

  2. Step 2The metric score48 → 71

    Oxford turns each value into a 0 to 100 score. Its formula isn't public, so we fitted a curve to its scores for all 1,000 cities. Cities already near 0.25 (Porto, Antwerp, Wellington, Canberra, Ghent) score 70.5 to 71.5.

  3. Step 3The pillar62.8 → 69.4

    This metric is 15% of Quality of Life, so Jacksonville's Quality of Life score rises.

  4. Step 4The overall score67.4 → 69.8

    Quality of Life is 25% of the total. These weights reproduce Oxford's published scores to within 0.001 points.

  5. Step 5The rank97th → 73rd

    Every other city keeps its 2026 score. At 69.8, Jacksonville passes 24 of them.

What it can't tell you

  • Ranks are estimates, usually good to within a few places. A second method (reading scores off the nearest real cities) agrees to within 5 places.
  • Other cities don't stand still. The calculator holds them at their 2026 scores; in reality they will move too.
  • Not every lever is local. Governance is set for the whole country, and educational attainment and life expectancy are measured for Florida. Natural disasters, climate, economic stability and age profile aren't things local action changes. You can move them to see their weight, but they are tagged so nobody mistakes them for an action item.
  • It shows the effect, not the effort. It can't say what a change would cost or how long it would take.
  • "Top 50 typical" is the median among the Top 50 cities under 4 million. On some levers that is a big ask: on public safety it means a homicide rate about 90% lower.
